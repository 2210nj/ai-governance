Assessing an AI resume-grading system

A governance case study by Namita, October 2026

“We are deploying an AI system that reviews and grades candidates’ resumes against a job opening. Walk me through how you would assess its impact.”

My approach has four steps: define the system and its legal status, map who it touches, rank the harms, and set safeguards that can be measured. I would run it with HR, legal, privacy, security and data science from the start, because an assessment done by governance alone misses how the tool is actually used.

0Scope and classification

Before assessing anything, I pin down three facts.

  • Intended use. Which decision does the score feed into, for which roles and which countries?
  • Our role. Did we build the system or buy it? This decides who owes which obligations and what evidence we can demand from a vendor.
  • Regulatory tier. Under Annex III of the EU AI Act, tools that filter applications and evaluate candidates are high-risk. The Digital Omnibus has moved the deadline for those obligations from August 2026 to 2 December 2027. GDPR and anti-discrimination law still apply today, so I would build to the high-risk standard now rather than treat the delay as breathing room.

1Understand the system

The first question is how much authority the system really has. A tool labelled “advisory” that recruiters follow almost every time is making the decision in practice. The more effective authority it has, the stronger the oversight must be.

  • Behaviour. Is there an auto-reject threshold? Are candidates ranked, filtered or only flagged?
  • Method. Is it keyword matching, a trained model or an LLM? What was it trained on, and does that data encode past hiring patterns?
  • Inputs. Which fields does it read, and could any of them stand in for a protected trait?
  • Evidence. I would request the documentation, vendor validation reports, earlier risk findings, any bias audits, and a sample of real scored resumes. I would also sit with recruiters and hiring managers to see how scores are used.

2Map the stakeholders

I think of stakeholders as concentric circles, from the people who operate the tool to the people who hold it accountable.

Applicants are the most affected group and have the least visibility and voice. For that reason I would design a way for them to ask for a human review, and I would not rely on recruiters to speak for them.

3Assess risks and severity

I score each risk on severity, likelihood, reversibility and scale. Automated rejection operates at high volume and is hard to see, so it ranks high even when each single error looks small.

4Recommend safeguards

Technical

  • Validate on a human-labelled test set before launch.
  • Report selection-rate ratios and error rates by group.
  • Run adversarial and injection tests.
  • Log every score with its rationale.

Procedural

  • Keep the system advisory, with no auto-reject.
  • Show recruiters why a score was given.
  • Audit a random sample of rejections regularly.
  • Tell candidates AI is used and let them request human review.

Organisational

  • Name a system owner with a clear RACI.
  • Hold periodic governance reviews.
  • Agree rollback triggers in advance, such as a selection-rate ratio falling below the set threshold.
  • Run an incident and flagging process for questionable scores.

I would roll out in stages: shadow mode first, then a pilot on a few roles, then wider use. Each stage has an exit criterion that must be met before the next begins.

My recommendation

A conditional go. Start in shadow mode and keep the tool advisory, with bias testing and human review of rejections. Move to wider use only if it meets fairness and accuracy thresholds agreed in advance, and only with a named owner and defined rollback triggers.
